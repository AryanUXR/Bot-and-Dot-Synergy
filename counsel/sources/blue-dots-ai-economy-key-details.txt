At a Glance
Across India's districts, people, businesses and service providers are not easily discoverable. Often within
a few kilometres of one another, they struggle to discover & connect. A young person looks for work and
support while an employer two bus stops away cannot fill a vacancy. A person with a disability searches
for a rehabilitation professional who practises nearby. A business looks for suppliers and services that
already exist in its own district, and a homestay or artisan waits for tourists who never hear of them. Much
of this activity is digitally dark: present on both sides, yet invisible to the other.
The Blue Dotsᴬᴵ Economy makes this local demand and supply discoverable to one another. A person,
business or provider says who they are, what they have and what they want in their own language, in a
voice conversation that takes about 1 minute 40 seconds and costs about ₹2, against around ₹500 and
two weeks through field surveys. That intent becomes a Blue Dot, a relevant, nearby signal that others
can discover and act on. The economic unlock follows from that. Engagements that were never worth the
effort start to happen, people find work, services and customers close to home, and income earned locally
is spent locally. Districts that can see and serve their own demand grow as economies in their own right,
less dependent on metros.
What has surfaced in a few places points to a far larger national opportunity. In each area, a local finding
says something specific about what is possible across the country.

Each of these was seen in a pocket: a district, a region, or one assessor's pool of candidates. Taken to
the scale of the country, the potential runs in two directions. The efficiency savings are large, because the
cost and time of finding each other fall for every person, business and provider involved. The productivity
gains are larger still, as work, services and talent that were sitting idle begin to be used. 200 key districts,
beyond the metros, are where this can be shown first.
Beyond India, the UAE Prime Minister's Office has become an advocate for the approach and will
convene high-level meetings to take it to African nations, and South Africa is applying it to one of its
engagements to grow micro-entrepreneurs. Investors are beginning to treat informal markets as an
investment category of their own.
1. Context
1.1 Local Economies That Are Invisible
India's local economies are full of people, businesses & services looking for each other. One young caller
asked not just for a job but for an employer who could also arrange lodging, food and commute, because
for youth, finding a job is just one of the several needs in their livelihoods journey, alongside counselling,
skilling, further study or capital to start something of their own. A family in Basti searches for someone to
offer an assistive device while a service that can help them avail it, is just a few kilometres away. MSME
owners in Ghaziabad travel to Delhi for services that businesses in their own district already offer. A
tourist in Udupi looks for a guide whose credentials they can check, or a surf operator they can be sure is
genuine, while local guides and operators have no way to be discovered beyond their own networks.
This is a paradox of proximity: demand and supply sit close together while remaining invisible to one
another. Both sides are often digitally dark. A business that hires by word of mouth and a job seeker who
has never written a CV cannot find each other, and neither can a family looking for a speech therapist and
the professional practising two kilometres away. National platforms rarely reach this activity, which is local,
low-ticket and more often spoken than written down.
1.2 Why Discovery Breaks Down
These connections fail because much of this activity is digitally dark: what people need and what others
offer is never recorded anywhere the other side can discover it, so it stays within word of mouth and
personal networks. Where discovery happens at all, it is slow and costly. Searching costs time and
money: asking around, travelling to offices and job fairs, scanning posters and WhatsApp groups.
Surfacing one person's intent through field surveys has cost around ₹500 and two weeks. Establishing
credibility is hard: two in three people answer calls only from numbers they recognise, and a tourist
approached by a self-described local guide has no way to check whether the guide is genuine. Reaching
an opportunity costs travel and time, which is why many women candidates look for work no further than
two bus stops from home.

=== page 4 ===
The late C.K. Prahalad, professor at the University of Michigan's Ross School of Business, argued in The
Fortune at the Bottom of the Pyramid (2004) that markets at the base of the economy go unserved not
because they lack value, but because serving them needs a different cost structure. Aneel Karnani,
professor of strategy at the same school, added that people in these markets create the most value as
producers and earners, not only as consumers. Together they describe what local economies need:
discovery that is cheap enough and wide enough for many small engagements to become viable. Cost is
only part of it. A service that is never discovered is never used, and an opening that is never discovered is
never filled, so each is a lost opportunity for both sides. Making both sides discoverable, with people
taking part as earners and providers as well as customers, is what expands these markets.
Several factors now make discovery both cheaper and wider. Proximity lowers the cost of reaching an
opportunity. Voice conversations in a person's own language make discovery possible for people who
have never filled a digital form, bringing them into markets they were previously outside. Local institutions
that people already recognise, such as district administrations, MSME associations, ITIs, NGOs and
self-help groups, lend credibility and bring participants in. A Blue Dot or a verified credential, once
created, can be reused across many future discoveries instead of being rebuilt each time. And shared
rails unbundle who is offering what, so any provider can respond to visible demand without first building
its own way of reaching customers. Together, these do more than save cost and time: they bring demand
and supply that were never discovered into the market, which is how local markets expand.
1.3 From Efficiency to Productivity
Lower discovery costs are a gain in themselves, in time and money saved by everyone searching. They
also raise productivity. Workers hired close to home spend less time travelling and more time working,
and they stay in their roles longer, so businesses spend less on repeated hiring and training. Matches
improve when needs are personalised and skills are verified, so fewer placements fail. Businesses that
find workers, suppliers and services nearby operate with lower costs and shorter lead times. People who
were willing but held back, such as women seeking assurance about safety and shifts, enter productive
activity once that information is visible. A 21-year-old first-time graduate in Ghaziabad, now working at a
store near her home, put the change simply: "I didn't know I could get something this quickly.
"
These gains stay local. When local candidates are hired by local enterprises, they earn locally and spend
locally, and the local economy benefits in turn. Ghaziabad's Chief Development Officer has described how
visible local opportunity can turn districts into counter-magnets to the steady migration towards cities. The
same logic extends well beyond jobs. MSMEs that trade and source services within their own district keep
that spending local, and tourist spending that reaches local guides, artisans and homestays circulates
where it is earned. As productivity rises across many such local pockets, India's dependence on a few
metros as its economic centres can begin to ease.
1.4 Personalised, Proximal Discovery as the Unlock
Personalised, proximal discovery is the answer to digital darkness. When a person, business or provider
can say in their own words what they have and what they want, and others nearby can find it, activity that
was invisible enters the economy: openings get filled, services find clients, toursits find hosts and
businesses find suppliers. It is personalised, so that what people find is relevant to their need, capability
and circumstances, since discovery without relevance would only add noise. It is proximal, so that what
people find is close enough to act on, with "close" defined by the person and the journey rather than a

=== page 5 ===
fixed radius. Repeated across districts and sectors, this turns hidden local demand and supply into
economic activity at national scale.
1.5 Why Now: DPI 2.0 and AI
Between 2010 and 2026, India's Digital Public Infrastructure gave a billion people identity, payments and
documents on shared public rails. NITI Aayog's April 2026 roadmap, DPI@2047 for Viksit Bharat, sets out
the next phase. DPI 2.0, from 2025 to 2035, is meant to drive livelihood-led growth across MSMEs,
agriculture, education, health and other sectors, moving India from digital inclusion to productivity-led
growth. The roadmap calls for aggregating demand through district-level programmes executed by states
and districts, and for expanding digital transactions through open, interoperable networks that unbundle
supply and demand. The roadmap also uses the term "blue dot" itself. In two of its sectoral
transformations,
"MSME Jobs Finding Local Talent" and "Benefits finding Beneficiaries"
, it describes
people broadcasting who they are and what they seek in a credible way as a "blue dot"
. The Blue Dotsᴬᴵ
Economy puts these principles into practice as an AI diffusion pathway, built as shared digital rails with
voice AI in the loop, and it represents both demand and supply in every use case.
AI is what makes personalised, proximal discovery viable in Tier 2 and Tier 3 India and beyond. A person
speaks in Hindi, Kannada or another language, and voice AI captures and structures what they have and
what they want. Calls average 1 minute 40 seconds, and what once took field teams weeks of travel and
many hours of effort now happens in a single conversation at a fraction of the cost. The average hides
something useful: when people have more to say, they keep talking. During campaigns, the district
administration in Ghaziabad observed high-intent seekers speaking with the voice agent for 10 minutes or
more, and a startup working with women entrepreneurs observed women opening up about their needs
because they knew they were not speaking to a person. As cost and time keep falling, it becomes
possible to digitally light up the aspirations of India's youth, women, businesses and service providers at
the pace its demographic dividend demands.
1.6 The Blue Dotsᴬᴵ Economy
As more local demand and supply becomes discoverable, an economy begins to emerge: a Blue Dotsᴬᴵ
Economy, in which the intent of people, businesses, providers and everyone in between is discoverable in
proximity across India's local economies. A Blue Dot is that intent, structured, consented and placed on a
shared local map, where relevant people and institutions nearby can find it and act on it.
Personalised, proximal discovery, in this sense, becomes the shared public rails that local services have
always lacked. A service provider no longer has to build its own way of finding customers, and a citizen in
any district can find the opportunity, support or service they need without travelling to a metro or relying
on chance. The implementations that follow show this taking shape across sectors.
2. Implementations Underway
Each implementation follows the same pattern of AI diffusion. It starts with a persona, a specific problem
of discovery and the economic unlock that solving it would bring. From there, it needs four things. An
institutional anchor from samaaj, sarkaar or bazaar takes ownership. A local ecosystem of institutions
people already recognise, such as MSME associations, ITIs and colleges, NGOs, self-help groups and
district departments, brings participants in. Enablers, local or not, including startups, training organisations

things together: voice AI agents that let people express intent in their own language, a shared map on
which that intent becomes discoverable, and open Digital Public Goods that structure, verify and connect
it. The approach is set out in more detail in Appendix A.

2.1 Livelihoods: Blue Dots
The Challenge
In Ghaziabad, national employment portals listed 97 local jobs; in Hubli-Dharwad, at the same point, only
four. Local businesses were hiring all the while, but their openings never reached a channel job seekers
could see, and job seekers had no way to show employers nearby what they could do.
The Response
The district administrations of Ghaziabad (Uttar Pradesh) and Hubli-Dharwad (Karnataka) anchored the
work, each through a Joint Facilitation Centre with nominated members from the ITI, higher education,
polytechnic, industry and employment exchange departments. Existing institutions became aggregators.
MSME associations, including AIMO and IIA in Ghaziabad and KCCI in Hubli, brought member
businesses on through Blue Dot-a-thons, and ITIs and colleges brought in their students. Employers and
job seekers express intent through a voice agent in Hindi or Kannada, or by scanning QR codes at job
fairs, and the district promotes the voice AI number itself. Startups and NGOs, including Proof of Skill,
RecEx, JobsUPI, TRRAIN and Head Held High Foundation, offer assessment, recruitment, counselling
and job-readiness services on the demand that becomes visible.
What Emerged
In Ghaziabad, fewer than 10% of MSMEs surfaced over 12,000 jobs, and 16,000 job seekers became
visible. In Hubli-Dharwad, 16,000 jobs and 8,000 candidates are on the map, and one MSME association
put nearly 900 jobs online in four hours. At one point during the pilot, the data showed electrician
vacancies outnumbering newly trained candidates by nearly 3 to 1, while trained youth were leaving for
Bengaluru.
Over 70% of applications went to opportunities within 5 km. Targeted placement drives converted at over
50%, against under 10% at broad job fairs, and candidate mobilisation that took months now takes days.
In Hubli-Dharwad, applications rose 300% once the district itself promoted the AI voice number and QR

=== page 8 ===
codes, and inbound callers applied at twice the rate of those reached by cold calls. When the voice agent
began completing applications within the call, the application failure rate fell from 88.6% to 2%.
The conversations went well beyond jobs. More than half of the youth who called asked for something
other than a job, naming over ten needs from counselling and skilling to higher education,
entrepreneurship, capital and machines. Women engaged with the voice bot as much as men but applied
at around one-third of the rate. A "women-friendly workplace" signal tripled their applications, and MSME
associations have begun marking and broadcasting women-friendly jobs. Aggregated call data also
showed that some factories start their first shift at 8:00 a.m. while the first buses from nearby areas arrive
at 9:00 a.m.

Economic Potential
If fewer than 10% of Ghaziabad's MSMEs surfaced over 12,000 jobs, the remaining 90% could make tens
of thousands more jobs digitally discoverable in that district alone. Nationally, MSMEs contribute around
31% of GDP and employ about 38.9 crore people, with 8.7 crore enterprises registered on the Udyam
portal (MSME fact sheet, June 2026), and over 1.1 crore students graduate each year (AISHE 2023-24).
If hiring demand across these businesses is as unseen as Ghaziabad's was, making it discoverable
connects that supply to work close to home, starting with India's top 200 districts. That changes where
people earn and spend. Candidates hired by local enterprises earn locally, spend locally and have less
reason to migrate to the metros, so districts keep both their talent and the income it generates, and that
spending sustains other local businesses in turn.
2.2 Disability: Purple Dots
The Challenge
Basti district alone has about 60,000 persons with disabilities, most of them with locomotor, visual or
hearing disabilities. Their needs range from assistive devices and rehabilitation to support that sits
beyond the disability itself, such as pensions, UDID cards and other schemes. Yet the wider ecosystem
that could meet these needs, including government departments, rehabilitation professionals, NGOs,
device providers, lenders and employers, has had no clear view of where these needs are, and persons
with disabilities have had no clear view of who nearby can help. Providers face the same gap from the
other side. ALIMCO's assistive-device camps have seen low turnout because information does not reach
eligible people, and NGOs with strong local reach have no list of who needs them.

=== page 9 ===
The Response
DEPwD and ALIMCO first ran a national campaign that Purple Dotted the needs of 1.1 lakh persons with
disabilities across the country. Building on what that campaign showed, DEPwD has begun rolling out
Purple Dots across 14 districts under an MoU signed in September 2026 for Purple Dots, the Unified
Benefits Interface, and aims to cover 30 districts this fiscal year. District administrations anchor the work
on the ground, beginning with Basti. Tech Mahindra Foundation and EnAble India implement it, and
EkStep Foundation seeded it.
There are two kinds of Purple Dots: a person with a disability or a caregiver who states a need, and a
provider of services, such as a rehabilitation professional, a prosthetics and orthotics or hearing-aid
provider, a therapist, or an NGO that helps with documentation and scheme applications, who can find
people by need and respond. RCI-registered professionals list their services with their registration number
at no cost, and contact is shared only once both sides consent. In Basti, 3 organisations and 15 self-help
groups and ASHA supervisors were shortlisted as local points of assistance for documentation, UDID
cards and assistive-device applications.
What Emerged
The district rollout has only just gone live, in Basti, so outcomes will emerge over the coming months. The
needs of Basti's persons with disabilities are being Purple Dotted, and the most requested so far are
prosthetics and orthotics, speech and hearing support, and access to schemes and services. District
ownership came early. After a stakeholder workshop set out what each actor could offer and needed from
the others, the Basti Health Officer observed that the technology was fine, but what mattered was seeing
how the programme would work on the ground, and the Chief Development Officer brought health,
welfare, rural development and skilling onto the district working committee.
What Next
●
●
Purple Dots will expand from 14 to 30 districts this fiscal year.
The next stage moves from access to participation, surfacing the intent of persons with disabilities
to work, contribute and earn, alongside the organisations and employers who can enable it.
Economic Potential
Basti alone generates a wide and specific set of needs. Nationally, the Census 2011 counted 2.68 crore
persons with disabilities, about 2.2% of the population, a figure widely regarded as conservative. When
DEPwD mapped needs nationally, locomotor, visual and hearing disabilities were the most common, and
demand spanned assistive devices, financial support such as loans and scholarships, and employment
and skilling. Mapped at national scale, these needs would give a live view of what persons with disabilities
need and where. That view becomes the canvas on which schemes, services and opportunities can be
delivered. Persons with disabilities then take part in the economy on both sides. As consumers, they
access the devices, therapy, training and financial products they need, which creates a large market for
rehabilitation professionals, device makers, lenders and trainers. As contributors, they find work, start
businesses and earn, adding to local output and income.

2.3 Education and Skilling: Yellow Dots
The Challenge
Each year, around 70 lakh people take regulator-recognised assessments for banking, financial services
and insurance, for certification, recruitment and promotion. Only a share of them are hired into BFSI roles.
The rest are not weak candidates; most were simply not the top candidate for one particular opening, and
their assessed skills then disappear from view. The same pattern runs through learning more widely.
Students looking for tuition, coaching and test preparation depend on known institutions, capable tutors
working from homes and neighbourhoods remain hard to find, and what a learner can do is rarely
recorded in a form they can carry and reuse. With the national ambition of equipping one crore young
people with AI skills, the volume of talent that needs to be visible will only grow.
The Response
The entire effort centres on building a Talent DPI, with mySATHI Foundation as anchor and CL Educate
and IIT Kharagpur as ecosystem partners. As a DPI, it is a set of shared rails, not one organisation's
platform: any organisation, institution or individual can bring learners, providers or services onto it. On
these rails, learners, tutors and credentialed talent become discoverable by location and capability.
Candidates assessed for BFSI roles can hold a BFSI credential that stays with them and appears on a
map, so that banks and other financial institutions can see where credentialed talent is concentrated in
the places where they hire or plan to expand. The same applies to young people trained in AI skills, and
to tutors and coaching providers who can be assessed, credentialed and found locally. Because
credentials are reusable, a learner can carry them to colleges, employers and later opportunities instead
of proving the same skill again.

Economic Potential
This pool is national from the start, and it renews every year: around 70 lakh people are assessed and
credentialed for BFSI roles. Today, the value of that assessment largely ends with a single hiring decision.
A credential that stays with the person and is placed on a map turns it into a lasting economic asset. For
employers, it lowers the cost and time of hiring: banks and other financial institutions can see where
credentialed talent already sits and hire locally, without sourcing, screening and assessing the same skills
again. For candidates, a reusable credential opens more opportunities closer to home, so assessed skills
are put to productive use instead of going idle. For districts, visible talent shapes where demand is
created: an institution that can see credentialed talent in a district can expand there with confidence,
bringing jobs, income and spending to places beyond the metros. The same applies to the national
ambition of equipping one crore young people with AI skills, so that the returns on that skilling are realised
where young people live.

2.4 Tourism, Arts and Culture: Orange Dots
The Challenge
Udupi has much of what tourists look for: the Krishna Matha, Yakshagana and Pili Vesha performance, a
distinctive cuisine and a coastal adventure culture. When OneTAC mapped the questions of seven kinds
of tourists, from solo travellers to families and international backpackers, their most common needs were
the hardest to meet: a surf lesson from an operator they could be sure was real, a guide whose
credentials they could check, a fair auto fare, a temple stay. The people who can meet these needs,
including guides, performers, artisans, boat operators, home cooks and hosts, are largely invisible online,
so the value they could earn flows to intermediaries or does not materialise at all.
The Response
OneTAC, a public, private and community-led framework for tourism, arts and culture, anchors Orange
Dots, working with the Udupi district administration and the tourism department. Orange Dots applies the
same discovery logic to experiences: where a Blue Dot makes a worker or service discoverable, an
Orange Dot makes a local experience discoverable and ties it to a verified, named practitioner. OneTAC
provides the standards, credibility and coordination layers that let local actors become discoverable and
transactable while staying locally owned. It is not a booking platform or a marketplace, and it is sustained
through CSR support as a commission-free ecosystem. Working with the Deputy Commissioner's office in
Udupi and the local ecosystem, including the tourism department, self-help groups, artisan and performer
communities and local entrepreneurs, a local OneTAC Hub carries newly visible actors through validation,
capacity building, market access and credit readiness. The district administration validates practitioners,
which gives tourists confidence in what they find.
For the demand side, simple ways in for tourists are being built: QR codes at bus stands, beaches,
temples, in autos and on practitioners' ID cards; a map of verified Orange Dots; and Tourist Saarthi, a
voice AI service that will call tourists back in their language with answers on guides, food, stays and local
conditions. Tourists will also be able to check a practitioner's ID to confirm they are verified.
What Emerged
More than 1,000 Orange Dots have been created across Udupi and coastal Karnataka. They span
guides, Yakshagana and folk performers, artisans, self-help-group culinary collectives, boat operators,
homestay hosts and food custodians, many of whom had never held a digital profile. The supply side
came first, so that when the demand side opens in November 2026, tourists will find a full picture of what
is on offer.

Economic Potential
A single region already holds thousands of experience providers that tourists have never been able to
find. Nationally, travel and tourism contributes 5.22% of India's GDP, about ₹15.73 lakh crore in 2023-24,
and supports 46.5 million jobs, according to PIB and WTTC figures. India's handicraft and handloom
artisans number between 64.66 lakh and 1.13 crore by different estimates, earning on average about
₹270 a day (Economic Survey 2025-26; Institute for Human Development and Crafts Council of India,
2025). Anecdotally, only around a fifth of such experiences and artisans are visible to tourists, yet that
visible share already sustains tourism's contribution to GDP. The rest is value that discovery could bring
into the economy, and into the hands of the people who carry a place's culture.
3. Emerging Use Cases
The same rails are being extended into further parts of local economies. Two are already taking shape;
others are set out in Appendix B.
3.1 Enterprise: Brown Dots
The Challenge
Businesses, service providers and entrepreneurs need markets, capital, suppliers, schemes and services
to grow, and much of what they need already exists in their own district. Yet they rarely know what is
available locally. MoSPI's Annual Survey of Unincorporated Sector Enterprises for 2025 counts 7.92 crore
establishments employing 12.81 crore workers, of which only 39.4% use the internet for business (MoSPI,
March 2026). Most of these enterprises are as hard to find as they find it hard to find others.
The Response
MSME associations and District Industries Centres are making enterprise needs discoverable. An
association aggregates what its members need, broadcasts it to providers, and brings credible providers
back to its members. AIMO in Ghaziabad declared its ambition to become the first 100% AI-enabled
MSME association and brought 75 member businesses together with startups, NGOs and the district
administration. In pilots with women entrepreneurs, including Youvati around Moradabad, the local
ecosystem was mapped to surface the services entrepreneurs needed close to home.
What Emerged
At the AIMO gathering, members answered questions by raising numbered placards, which revealed
capabilities the association had not known about: around 40 members already employed women, one of
them 200 among 500 workers. Once demand was aggregated, providers competed for it. A recruitment
startup offered the first 20 MSMEs a 20% discount, shortlisting within 48 hours and a 90-day replacement
guarantee. In pilots with women entrepreneurs, including one run by Youvati, a startup from the
Bharat10M accelerator cohort, anchored by Caret Capital that supports women-led businesses around
Moradabad and Bijnor, the local ecosystem was mapped to surface the services entrepreneurs needed
close to home.

3.2 Welfare and Public Services: Golden Dots
The Challenge
Citizens often struggle to discover the welfare schemes, skilling opportunities, livelihood support and
public services they may already be eligible for. Many departments and ecosystem organisations run
valuable programmes, but the system is fragmented, and data on beneficiaries and services sits in
separate departments. Eligible people miss out, and programmes reach fewer of the people they were
designed for.
The Response
Golden Dots, under discussion with a state government, would create structured, credible and verifiable
signals across beneficiaries, schemes, services and ecosystem actors. Citizens would discover what fits
them through voice bots, WhatsApp, local facilitation centres and ecosystem partners, with guidance on
the schemes and benefits they are eligible for, skilling and livelihood opportunities, healthcare and
disability support, documentation and application requirements, and support providers nearby. NGOs,
CSR organisations, foundations, healthcare partners, skilling providers and welfare institutions can
themselves become discoverable and offer counselling, assessments, documentation support and other
last-mile services. Self-help groups, ASHA supervisors and similar community-based organisations would
carry this discovery to the last mile. They already exist in villages and wards across the country, they
carry credibility with citizens, and they know which households are eligible for support but are not
receiving it. A citizen is far more likely to state a need, or to answer a call, when someone they recognise
is involved. Purple Dots has already shown this in Basti, where 15 self-help groups and ASHA
supervisors were shortlisted as local points of assistance for documentation, UDID cards and
assistive-device applications. Golden Dots would extend that role, with these groups helping citizens state
their needs, confirm their eligibility and complete their applications, so that discovery spreads through
networks that are already in place rather than ones that have to be built. The same Digital Public Goods
that power Blue Dots and Purple Dots would serve as the foundation, configured by an implementation
partner and run through a state-level PMU and district facilitation structures.
Economic Potential
When benefits and services reach the citizens they were meant for, public spending does more. Today,
scheme benefits can reach people who are not eligible while eligible citizens miss out. Verified,
continuously updated signals on who is eligible reduce that leakage in disbursement, so the same budget
reaches more of the right people. And because citizens are connected to skilling, livelihood support and
services close to home, more of them can earn locally and spend that income in their own district, instead
of migrating to find opportunities elsewhere. Interoperable, continuously updated signals across
departments also give the state a significant public data unlock: better planning, convergence across
departments, less duplication and more efficient allocation of public resources. Working alongside Blue
Dots and Purple Dots, Golden Dots would let a citizen discover welfare support, skilling pathways,
livelihood opportunities and local services in one place.

4. Markets Building on Shared Rails
To investors, Tier 2 and Tier 3 markets have long looked informal: high in volume, low in ticket size and
too costly to reach. Venture capital has largely stayed away for that reason, not for lack of demand. As
one investor told the team, you cannot offer services in a place until the roads are built.
The two accelerators run with Caret Capital and Headstart Network Foundation set out to test whether
shared discovery rails change that. Startups applied to both accelerators through the Bharat Pitchathon,
Headstart Network Foundation's flagship programme for surfacing founders from across Bharat, not just
the metros. Of 568 applicants, 80 were longlisted, 26 were shortlisted to join the two cohorts and 12 ran
pilots in Tier 2 and Tier 3 districts.The pilots tested two questions: does personalised, proximal discovery
lower the cost and time of acquiring customers enough to make these markets viable, and does it surface
market opportunities that were not visible before? The evidence says yes on both counts. Baylink sourced
gig workers within 2.5 km of the work location more cheaply and quickly than through the open market,
and they stayed longer in the role. Digital Labour Chowk, matching workers and opportunities directly,
found that workers preferred opportunities within about 3 km of home. Proof of Skill's AI mock interviews
in Ghaziabad led employers to approach candidates instead of the other way round. In each case,
startups also saw a new market opportunity for them in the long tail of the economy, markets that were
not legible earlier.
5. Learnings from Implementation
The same patterns recur across livelihoods, disability, education, tourism and enterprise. The first four
learnings concern what personalised, proximal discovery reveals, the next five what it changes
economically, and the last four what makes it work and keeps it working.
5.1 Demand and Supply Already Exist but Remain Unseen
The first constraint in a local economy is often visibility, not the absence of jobs, services, talent or
experiences. In every implementation, demand and supply were present before they were discoverable:
openings that MSMEs held but no portal listed, persons with disabilities waiting for providers already
practising in their district, guides and artisans operating outside any tourist’s view, and businesses
unaware of services available next door. Even the institutions closest to a market may not see it until they
ask.
5.2 Speaking Reveals Demand That Forms Miss
Systems designed around one transaction capture one need. When people speak in their own language
instead of filling a form, they say more, and conversations run longer when there is more to say. The Blue
Dots approach was built around the same reality, since one person may need a pension, a device,
therapy and a scheme at once. Each additional need captured, or service provider added, took only 10 to
20% extra effort on top of the existing infrastructure and approach.

=== page 15 ===
5.3 Livelihoods is beyond helping someone find a Job
Many people are looking for a livelihood, and a job is only one route to it. One candidate said,
"I don't
need a job, I need machines and a loan to grow my work.
" Counselling, skilling, assessments and
entrepreneurship are livelihood pathways in their own right, and each is also a service someone can
provide. Among first-time graduates, 80% apply only once they know an opportunity is nearby, the right fit,
offers growth and pays fairly for the area, and "more guidance" was the leading reason around 38% of
high-intent youth did not apply. The same holds for persons with disabilities: alongside support and
services, discovery surfaces their intent to participate, contribute and earn. Someone who upskills from
a ₹15,000 role to a local job that pays ₹20,000 earns, spends and contributes more locally.
Someone who starts a local business creates income, and often livelihoods for others. Much of
the economic return lies in these pathways, not only in placements.
5.4 Discovery Exposes Participation Gaps
Making intent visible does not guarantee that people act on it, but it shows why they do not. 2/3rd of
women held back until they had assurance about safety, proximity, amenities and shifts. Many MSMEs
already hire women; what they lack is a common standard for what a women-friendly or safe workplace
means in the places they run, and associations are now asking for guidance and advisory on it. Persons
with disabilities face a similar gap, eligible for schemes that never reach them. In each case, what looked
like low interest was unmet demand, and the response it called for was specific and often inexpensive.
5.5 Proximity Changes the Economics of Participation
What counts as local depends on the person and the journey, and it is often very local. Baylink's pilot
landed on 2.5 km; as its CEO put it,
"A candidate sourced in a 2.5 km radius from the work location is way
stickier than someone who has to travel more.
" Digital Labour Chowk saw workers prefer opportunities
within about 3 km, and Record heard the same pattern from its client during its skill-verification pilot. For
internships, NetPractice found that offers involving less than 30 minutes of travel were accepted, while
those beyond it were not, which matters most for interns who earn little and face family pressure over long
commutes. Purple Dots matches people and providers by need and distance together for the same
reason.
5.6 Earning and Spending Locally Strengthens Local Economies
Once local opportunity is visible, it competes with migration. Illustratively, a job paying ₹17,000 to ₹18,000
near home can leave more disposable income than a ₹20,000 job in a metro once rent, food and
commuting are paid, and that income is then spent in the local economy. Many male candidates now
search within their own district instead of migrating, which lets districts keep their own talent. The same
holds for businesses sourcing services locally and for tourist spending that reaches local hosts and
artisans.
5.7 Lowering the Cost of Discovery Expands Participation
At survey-level costs, reaching the long tail of local demand was uneconomic. At the cost of a short voice
call, it is viable, including for people who have never filled a digital form. Removing steps matters as much
as lowering cost. Job seekers who were once asked to complete applications later, on their own, mostly

=== page 16 ===
never did; once the call itself completed the application, the effort converted. Habits change once forms
are gone: MSMEs in Hubli-Dharwad now update their vacancies through a short AI call.
5.8 The Cost and Time of Acquiring Customers Fall
Across implementations, those who serve local demand spend less, and less time, finding the people they
serve. Discovery time falls from over two weeks to under a day. Training organisations in Hubli-Dharwad
cut mobilisation costs by up to 80%, startups source within a few kilometres at lower cost, and pooled
demand from MSME associations brought better terms from providers: aggregation creates visibility, and
volume creates negotiating power. Startups also did not have to build relationships and local context from
scratch; they built on what district departments and MSME associations had already aggregated, which
cut the time, effort and cost of entering a new geography. Rehabilitation professionals list on Purple Dots
at no cost, and Orange Dots hosts become findable without paying intermediaries. Lower acquisition cost
is what makes serving low-ticket local demand viable, and it is the clearest signal investors have
responded to.
5.9 Credentials Make Talent Legible and Can Shift Where Demand Is
Created
When talent is credentialed and visible on a map, employers can see what exists in a place before they
commit to it. That matters as companies look beyond metros. Nearly 28% of new Global Capability
Centres set up in 2023-24 were located in Tier 2 cities; Bosch's engineering and IoT operations in
Coimbatore and Schneider Electric's industrial automation centre in Vadodara are early examples, and
Madhya Pradesh's GCC Policy 2025 aims to bring more than 50 GCCs and 37,000 jobs to Indore and
Bhopal (Outlook Business). Credentialed, discoverable talent gives employers of every size the depth of
visibility to set up in a district with confidence. As more demand is created where talent already lives, its
concentration in a few metros can begin to ease.
5.10 Discovery is Augmented by Credibility
Because most people answer only numbers they recognise, and cold AI calls plateaued at around 30%
pick-up, discovery travels through institutions people already know: a district administration promoting its
own voice AI number, an association whose members follow its lead, a self-help group or ASHA
supervisor in a village. The channel has to fit the institution, such as QR codes at job fairs for skilling
departments, alumni WhatsApp groups for colleges and kiosks at district offices. A separate inbound
number that has not been marked as spam, with the institution named at the start of the call, keeps that
credibility intact. Institutions take ownership when it builds their standing, as AIMO's ambition to become
the first 100% AI-enabled MSME association showed. Credibility matters as much in what people find,
and here too the local ecosystem does much of the work by verifying that listings are genuine. MSME
associations ensure that only registered MSMEs post legitimate jobs, and skilling organisations verify the
candidates they bring onto the rails. Registration numbers on Purple Dots provider listings, verified
practitioners behind every Orange Dot and skill credentials on Yellow Dots all serve the same purpose:
signals people can act on without re-checking.

5.11 Aggregated Demand Becomes Local Economic Intelligence
Anonymised and aggregated, individual conversations show administrators what their local economy is
asking for and what is holding it back. A mismatch between factory shift timings and bus timetables in
Hubli-Dharwad surfaced this way. The same conversations showed what women look for before applying:
signals on safety, proximity, amenities and shifts, and details about the employer. In Basti, aggregated
requests show which disability services are most needed. The same intelligence shapes implementation:
knowing that "local" means 2.5 to 3 km in one place and a travel time in another tells teams how to curate
opportunities, where to focus outreach and how to plan the next phase. As DEPwD maps needs across
districts and OneTAC builds a verified experience registry, this intelligence extends to disability services
and tourism.
5.12 Technology Is 30% of the Work; the Other 70% Is the Local Ecosystem
Administrators can read a discovery initiative as a technology project. Adoption followed once the
execution was visible: in Ghaziabad and Hubli-Dharwad a structure (Joint Facilitation Centre) that set out
each actor's role, offers and needs moved the conversation from what the technology would do to how the
programme would run on the ground. Technology accounts for around 30% of what it takes to reach
scale, in line with findings from India's work on AI diffusion. The other 70% goes into activating the local
ecosystem and enablers, measuring, tracking and improving what happens on the ground, and setting up
the weekly and monthly governance rhythms that turn new practices into habits, such as Ghaziabad &
Hubli-Dharwad's department-wise goals and weekly reviews through its Joint Facilitation Centre.
5.13 The Value of Discovery Extends Beyond the First Connection
Needs change over time. A worker may later need training, an MSME may first need a worker and then a
supplier or capital, a person with a disability may need different support as circumstances change, and a
tourist who finds one local experience may look for others. Because a Blue Dot can be reused, people
and businesses keep finding what is relevant as their needs change, without starting over.
6. The Opportunity Ahead
India's DPI journey is moving from foundational inclusion towards broader economic participation,
productivity and inclusive prosperity. The implementations point to a gap between economic activity and
its discoverability. Across livelihoods, disability, education, tourism and enterprise, people, businesses
and providers already have intent and capability, but much of it remains scattered across institutions,
informal networks and individual knowledge.
The Blue Dotsᴬᴵ Economy offers a replicable way to close that gap across sectors and geographies. As
implementations grow, the opportunity is to connect them through interoperable public rails, so that a
signal created in one ecosystem can be discovered and acted on in another, and to treat discovery as
public infrastructure in the way identity and payments became.
The economic case follows directly. Making a market visible expands it: demand and supply that were
never discovered enter the economy, so services get used, openings get filled and providers find
customers they could not reach before. Lower discovery costs save time and money for every participant.
They make low-ticket, high-volume local markets viable for service providers and investors. They raise

=== page 18 ===
productivity through closer, better-matched and longer-lasting connections. And they keep income and
spending in the districts where it is earned, so that local economies grow in their own right and become
counter-magnets to migration rather than sources of it. Across hundreds of districts, that is how India's
demographic dividend can be realised close to where its young people, women and entrepreneurs
already live.
The opportunity is not to create a new economy, but to make more of the economy that already exists
visible, discoverable and connectable, so that proximity becomes an opportunity across India, not only in
its metros.
Appendices
Appendix A: Approach to Implementation
Each implementation follows a common approach, reflecting a broader lesson from AI diffusion:
technology is one part of reaching scale, and institutions and ecosystems carry much of the rest.
Persona and Problem
The starting point is a specific person, business or organisation and a concrete problem of discovering
demand or supply: a young person looking for work nearby, a person with a disability seeking a
prosthetist, a student seeking a tutor, a homestay looking for tourists, or a business looking for local
services. This determines whose intent needs to be captured, what proximity means in that context and
what a useful connection looks like.
Institutional Anchor
Each implementation needs an actor from samaaj, sarkaar or bazaar that can anchor it locally. District
administrations anchor livelihoods in Ghaziabad and Hubli-Dharwad, DEPwD and the Basti district
administration anchor Purple Dots, mySATHI Foundation anchors Yellow Dots, OneTAC anchors Orange
Dots, and MSME associations carry Brown Dots. The anchor brings relationships, local knowledge and
the ability to mobilise participants, and creates ownership that sustains the work beyond the first
implementation.
Ecosystem
The anchor works with the ecosystem needed to act on the demand and supply that emerges: MSME
associations, ITIs and colleges, NGOs, self-help groups and ASHA supervisors, rehabilitation
professionals, tutors, guides and artisans, training organisations, startups and service providers. These
actors act as aggregators, bringing people and businesses in through channels they already run, and as
providers, responding to demand once it is visible.
Solution
The final element is the technology that makes discovery work: voice AI agents that let people express
intent in their own language, mechanisms for structuring, consenting and verifying signals, location-aware
discovery on a shared map, and rails that connect participants. Where these capabilities serve common

=== page 19 ===
needs across contexts, they are built as Digital Public Goods, so each new implementation can reuse
them instead of building its own.

Appendix C: Digital Public Goods for Discovery
Each new implementation builds on reusable components instead of building its own infrastructure. The
Signal component handles registration, profile completion, checking, discovery and connection, giving
every participant one structure: who I am, what I want, what I have. The Aggregator component lets
institutions that already hold relationships, such as ITIs, MSME associations, departments and NGOs, run
assisted or bulk onboarding, verification and ongoing tracking. AI voice agents provide a spoken interface
that can be adapted to each participant type and works across voice AI providers. The map interface is
open source and can be integrated with any platform.
Together these support one recurring pattern: an existing relationship leads to aggregation and
onboarding, then to an expression of current intent, a structured and consented signal, verification,
discovery and connection. Scaling across ecosystems will need common standards for schemas,
identifiers, consent, credentials, verification and interoperability alongside these components.
